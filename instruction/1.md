# Research Task: Dynamic Internal Precipitation vs. Prat (2026) Analytical Growth Law

## 1. Research objective

Develop a reproducible, one-dimensional numerical model to investigate whether internal salt precipitation and the resulting pore-structure evolution alter the evaporation-driven growth law derived by Marc Prat (2026).

Reference paper:

**Prat, M. (2026). The Evaporation-Driven Growth Law of Porous Salt Structures. Langmuir.**

Source PDF:

`/mnt/data/Prat - 2026 - The Evaporation-Driven Growth Law of Porous Salt Structures.pdf`

Locate and read the source PDF before implementing the model. Verify the original governing equations, moving-interface conditions, assumptions, and parameter definitions.

### Central scientific question

Prat's model includes an internal precipitation reaction but derives its analytical growth law under approximately constant structural properties.

What happens if internal precipitation progressively modifies the porosity, specific surface area, and effective diffusivity?

Does the resulting moving-interface growth velocity differ significantly from the original analytical prediction?

We want to investigate this possibility, not assume the answer.

## 2. Implementation principles

Use Python, NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib.

Create an isolated research directory named `prat_dynamic_growth/` or an equivalent project directory.

Do not modify existing PNM models or their main branches.

Implement a well-documented, testable, conservative numerical model.

Prefer an implicit finite-volume solver because the precipitation kinetics may be stiff and the concentration boundary layer extremely thin.

Do not substitute the Prat analytical growth law for the interface velocity in the dynamic reference model.

The interface velocity must be computed independently from the interfacial mass balances and crystallization kinetics.

Separate numerical verification from physical validation.

## 3. Model A — Original Prat analytical prediction

Implement the original analytical growth law:

\[
v_A=\frac{dh_A}{dt}
=
\frac{C_{\mathrm{eq}}j}
{\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}(1-\varepsilon_s)(1-C_{\mathrm{eq}})}
\frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Da}^*}}{1+\sqrt{\mathrm{Da}^*}}
\]

where:

\[
\mathrm{Da}^*
=
\frac{\beta^2(1-\varepsilon_s)^2(1-C_{\mathrm{eq}})k_r}
{\varepsilon_sD_s^{\mathrm{eff}}a_v}
\]

Use the original parameters and hold the structural properties constant.

Also implement the more general analytical expression that retains:

\[
\tanh(\sqrt{\mathrm{Da}})
\]

This allows us to distinguish errors caused by the large-Da approximation from errors caused by evolving microstructure.

Integrate the analytical growth velocity to obtain \(h_A(t)\).

## 4. Model B — Dynamic internal precipitation and moving interface

Implement the original conservative governing equations rather than directly imposing the analytical growth law.

### Dissolved salt conservation

\[
\frac{\partial(\rho\varepsilon_s C)}{\partial t}
+
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}
\left(
\rho CU-
\rho\varepsilon_sD_s^{\mathrm{eff}}\frac{\partial C}{\partial z}
\right)
=-R_{\mathrm{int}}
\]

### Liquid solution mass conservation

\[
\frac{\partial(\rho\varepsilon_s)}{\partial t}
+
\frac{\partial(\rho U)}{\partial z}
=-R_{\mathrm{int}}
\]

### Internal precipitation kinetics

\[
R_{\mathrm{int}}
=
\rho a_vk_r(C-C_{\mathrm{eq}})
\]

### Porosity evolution

\[
-\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}\frac{\partial\varepsilon_s}{\partial t}
=
R_{\mathrm{int}}
\]

These expressions should be consistent with the paper's original volume-averaged equations.

Allow precipitation and dissolution according to the reaction law; avoid arbitrary clipping that violates mass conservation.

### Moving upper boundary

The domain is:

\[
0\le z\le h_B(t)
\]

At the bottom, impose:

\[
C(0,t)=C_{\mathrm{eq}}
\]

Represent the bottom as a saturated liquid reservoir capable of supplying liquid. Determine the required inflow consistently through liquid mass conservation rather than independently prescribing an incompatible flow rate.

At the top, prescribe evaporation mass flux \(j(t)\), and solve the coupled liquid mass balance, dissolved salt mass balance, and interfacial crystallization kinetics.

The interface crystallization relation is:

\[
\frac{dh_B}{dt}
=
\beta\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}}
k_r(C_{\mathrm{top}}-C_{\mathrm{eq}})
\]

Initially use \(\beta=2\), consistent with the cubic crystal model in the paper.

Use the original moving-interface liquid and salt mass balances from the paper, including the moving-boundary terms. The vapor-density contribution may be neglected if this approximation is documented.

Do not prescribe \(dh_B/dt\) using Model A.

### Critical moving-boundary requirement

Implement the moving domain using a conservative ALE finite-volume method, or a conservative fixed-grid method that adds new cells as the interface grows.

Account for mesh motion and newly created volume correctly.

In particular, the Eulerian porosity evolution equation must not be applied unchanged at fixed normalized coordinates \(z^*=z/h(t)\).

If using ALE coordinates, account for the mesh-motion contribution to the time derivative.

Track the porosity of newly formed layers separately from subsequent precipitation into existing layers.

Avoid double-counting salt that creates new layers and salt that precipitates inside previously formed material.

## 5. Dynamic microstructural properties

Start with a uniform structure:

\[
\varepsilon_s(z,0)=0.43
\]

After each time step, update the local porosity according to the precipitation reaction.

Define explicitly the porosity assigned to a newly created porous salt layer. Initially use the reference formation porosity \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{birth}}=0.43\), while allowing previously created layers to evolve.

Investigate two structural closures.

**Closure B1 — Frozen constitutive coefficients**

Allow porosity to evolve through precipitation but initially keep the reference \(a_v\) and \(D_s^{\mathrm{eff}}\) unchanged.

Treat this as a controlled sensitivity experiment, not a complete physical pore-structure model.

**Closure B2 — Updated constitutive coefficients**

Update the effective diffusivity using the paper's approximate relationship:

\[
D_s^{\mathrm{eff}}=\varepsilon_s^{1/2}D_s
\]

As an initial constitutive hypothesis, update the specific surface area using:

\[
a_v=\frac{36(1-\varepsilon_s)}{d_{\mathrm{sub}}}
\]

Do not assume this relationship is universally valid under crystal growth. State clearly that it is an exploratory closure that may fail when crystal sizes or morphologies change.

Compare B1 and B2 to identify the impact of constitutive updates.

Do not introduce permeability-controlled evaporation or hydraulic disconnection in the initial implementation. Keep the evaporation flux identical between models.

A later extension may add permeability and capillary supply limits.

## 6. Determining where internal precipitation occurs

Do not assign an arbitrary fixed-thickness precipitation zone.

At every time step, calculate:

\[
R_{\mathrm{int}}(z,t)
=
\rho a_v(z,t)k_r
[C(z,t)-C_{\mathrm{eq}}]
\]

The spatial distribution of precipitation must emerge from the computed concentration field.

For the original homogeneous, large-Da model, compare with the predicted concentration boundary-layer scale:

\[
\delta_C=
\sqrt{
\frac{\varepsilon_sD_s^{\mathrm{eff}}}
{a_vk_r(1-C_{\mathrm{eq}})}
}
\]

Use the reference prediction:

\[
R_{\mathrm{int}}(z,t)
\propto
\exp\left(-\frac{h(t)-z}{\delta_C}\right)
\]

only as an analytical comparison, not as an imposed reaction distribution in Model B.

Quantify the depth \(L_{95}\) containing 95% of the instantaneous integrated internal precipitation.

Investigate whether this region moves with the upper interface.

If the computed boundary layer becomes smaller than the salt crystal size or representative continuum scale, report that limitation explicitly.

### Stage B — Fully coupled evolving-porosity model

Enable conservative transient transport, internal precipitation, and moving-interface growth.

Update porosity and constitutive properties.

Compute the new interface position independently.

Compare Model B against Model A under identical initial conditions, evaporation flux, and material parameters.

### Stage C — Numerical robustness

Because the reaction layer can be submicrometer in thickness, use adaptive or strongly refined spatial resolution near the moving interface.

Check several spatial and temporal resolutions.

Use an implicit stiff solver and monitor nonlinear convergence.

Do not present unresolved boundary layers as converged solutions.

If a fully conservative solver cannot be implemented reliably, report the precise limitation rather than replacing it with an undocumented shortcut.

## 9. Critical mass-conservation audit

Implement rigorous global liquid-plus-solid mass and total salt mass checks.

For a unit cross-sectional area, define:

\[
M_{\mathrm{salt}}(t)
=
\int_0^{h(t)}
[
\rho\varepsilon_s C+
\rho_{\mathrm{cr}}(1-\varepsilon_s)
]\,dz
\]

When the growing top boundary does not export salt, the change in total salt mass must equal the net salt influx through the bottom boundary.

Use the total bottom salt flux, including both advective and diffusive contributions.

Similarly, audit total solution-plus-solid mass against bottom mass influx minus evaporation loss, under the stated density assumptions.

Maintain separate inventories for:

- Solid salt incorporated when new porous layers form.
- Additional solid salt precipitated within previously formed layers.
- Dissolved salt remaining in the liquid.
- Net salt transferred through the bottom reservoir.

Use these actual inventories to calculate any precipitation partition fraction.

Do not assume in advance that the fraction \(1-f(\mathrm{Da}^*)\) equals the physical mass fraction deposited inside existing pores. Investigate whether this interpretation is justified.
